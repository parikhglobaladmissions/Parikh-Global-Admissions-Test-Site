College Admissions Trends to Watch in 2026

What the data from this cycle actually means for your family's strategy.

NexGen Advising — July 27, 2026

Every admissions cycle produces a new round of headlines, and it is tempting to tune them out as noise. But 2026 is a year where the underlying data has genuinely shifted, not just the commentary around it. Application volume is up. Testing requirements are back at schools that dropped them years ago. And the strategies that worked for an older sibling two or three years ago no longer reliably apply.

For families in Florida and across the country, understanding what has actually changed, versus what just sounds dramatic, is the difference between a strategy grounded in fact and one built on outdated assumptions.

Application Volume Is Climbing, Not Slowing

According to the Common Application's March 2026 report, more than 1.4 million distinct first-year applicants applied to the 911 returning member institutions this cycle, a 2% increase over the same point last year. Total application volume rose even faster, up 5% year over year to more than 9.4 million submissions. Applicants are also spreading their applications wider: the average student applied to 6.59 schools this cycle, compared to 6.41 the year before.

For families, the implication is straightforward. A "safety school" strategy built around modest application counts is increasingly out of step with how the applicant pool actually behaves. More students are applying to more schools, which puts additional pressure on every list, at every selectivity level, including the ones families assume are close to a sure thing.

Standardized Testing Is Back, and Not Just at a Handful of Schools

The single biggest reversal of the past two years is the return of required standardized testing at some of the country's most selective institutions. Harvard, MIT, Yale, Princeton, Stanford, Brown, Dartmouth, Cornell, Georgetown, and Caltech have all reinstated SAT or ACT requirements, alongside institutions like Ohio State and the University of Tennessee System. The UC system remains the notable exception, staying fully test-blind.

The Common App's own data confirms the shift is broad, not isolated: this was the first admissions cycle since 2019–2020 in which more applicants submitted test scores than withheld them, with 52% of applicants reporting scores. Families who assumed the test-optional era was a permanent fixture are now recalibrating mid-cycle, which is exactly the position no one wants to be in during junior year.

Early Decision Is Rebounding, and Reshaping the Calendar

Early Decision applications, which dipped slightly during the 2024–25 cycle as testing requirements returned, have rebounded sharply this year. Bowdoin College, for example, saw Early Decision I and II applications rise 15% for the incoming class compared to the year before. Colleges continue to lean on ED as their primary tool for managing yield, which means the binding early round is becoming an even more central, and more strategic, part of the admissions calendar.

This matters for timeline planning specifically. A student who is not testing-ready, essay-ready, and list-ready by the fall of senior year is functionally opting out of the round where many selective schools now fill the largest share of their class.

What This Means for Florida Families Specifically

Florida families face a slightly different calculus than families in states with fewer strong public options. Florida's public university system, especially when paired with Bright Futures, remains one of the most cost-effective paths to a strong degree in the country. But rising national application volume means competition for admission and for merit aid at Florida's flagship institutions has intensified as well, not just at out-of-state reach schools.

The practical takeaway: a strong in-state option is no longer a guaranteed fallback. It deserves the same level of strategic preparation as a highly selective out-of-state reach.

Testing Strategy Has to Restart for Many Families

Families with a sophomore or freshman who assumed testing was optional now need a plan. That means figuring out early whether a student's target schools require scores, understanding that most schools superscore within a single test format (digital SAT to digital SAT, or ACT to ACT) but not always across formats, and building in multiple testing windows so a single sitting is never the entire strategy.

Waiting until junior spring to figure this out is the single most common and most avoidable mistake families are making this cycle.

The College List Needs to Be Built Differently This Year

With application volume rising across every selectivity tier, the old "reach, match, safety" framework needs a more rigorous approach behind it. A match school today may behave more like a reach compared to two years ago, simply because it is receiving more applications from a stronger pool. Lists built on last cycle's admit rates are already out of date.

Final Thought: Data Should Drive Strategy, Not Headlines

The temptation every admissions cycle is to react to whichever headline crossed a parent's feed that week. The more useful approach is to look at what the data actually shows: more applicants, more competition at every tier, testing requirements returning at a meaningful number of top schools, and Early Decision playing a larger role than it has in years.

Families who build their strategy around these realities, rather than around what was true when an older sibling applied, put their student in a materially stronger position.

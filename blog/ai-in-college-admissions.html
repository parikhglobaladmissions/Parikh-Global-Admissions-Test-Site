AI in College Admissions: What Families Need to Know

Where the real line is, and where families get it wrong.

NexGen Advising — July 24, 2026

Artificial intelligence has moved from an abstract concern to a daily reality inside the admissions process, on both sides of the desk. Students are using it. Admissions offices are using it. And the rules governing where the line sits are still being written in real time, which puts families in the uncomfortable position of making decisions before the norms are fully settled.

Here is where things actually stand, based on the data, not the anxiety.

Students Are Already Using AI, at Scale

The numbers here are not marginal. About half of college applicants report using AI to brainstorm their college essay, and one in five use it to create a first draft. In a broader survey of current undergraduate and graduate students, nearly half, 47%, said they would have used tools like ChatGPT to help complete their college admissions essays had those tools been available when they applied. Usage skews notably by generation: 62% of millennials say they would have turned to AI for their essay, compared to 39% of Gen Z, students closer to today's applicant pool.

Not Every Student Uses AI the Same Way, or for the Same Reasons

The data reveals a more complicated picture than "students are cheating." Lower-income students, represented in research by those receiving application fee waivers, were more likely to use AI in their essays, suggesting access to human editing support and paid essay coaching plays a role in who reaches for AI tools and why. Older applicants, first-generation college students, and men were also more likely to report they would use AI assistance. This complicates any simple narrative about AI use in admissions essays being purely about shortcuts.

Colleges Are Building AI Into Their Own Process

Families should understand this cuts both ways: 80% of colleges surveyed reported plans to incorporate AI into their own admissions review process. That includes AI-assisted first-pass reading, application volume triage, and in some cases, AI detection tools aimed at identifying AI-generated content in essays. The Common App has updated its own fraud policy to explicitly classify submitting AI-generated content as a student's own original work as academic fraud, a serious designation with real consequences for an application.

Detection Is Real, But Imperfect

Colleges reading tens of thousands of essays a cycle increasingly rely on both human pattern recognition and software tools to flag essays that read as generic, formulaic, or inconsistent with a student's other writing samples, like in-class essays or short answers. These tools are not perfect and can produce false positives on strong student writing that simply reads as polished. The safest position for a student is not to write in a way that appears "AI-proof," but to write in a way that is authentically theirs: specific, personal, and consistent with how they actually think and speak.

Where Families Get the Line Wrong

The mistake families most commonly make is treating this as binary: either a student writes every word alone with zero assistance, or they've crossed an ethical line. In practice, using AI to brainstorm topics, organize thoughts, or check grammar is fundamentally different from using it to generate the substance of a personal statement. The clearest, safest standard: AI can be a tool a student uses on their own writing, the way a thesaurus or an outline template might be, but it cannot become the author of the content itself, particularly the personal narrative and voice that admissions officers are specifically trying to evaluate.

Students Are Split on Whether This Is Fair

The majority of college students, 56%, believe using AI tools on college applications gives some applicants an unfair advantage. But a meaningful minority, 39%, believe AI tools could actually improve access and opportunity for traditionally underserved students, who may otherwise lack access to expensive private essay coaching. Both perspectives have merit, and it is exactly why families should not assume every college, or every admissions officer, holds an identical view of where acceptable use ends.

Final Thought: The Essay Is Still the One Place AI Can't Do the Work

Every part of the admissions process that AI touches, research, brainstorming, organization, grammar, still requires a student's authentic voice at the center of the final product, especially in the essay. Colleges are explicitly building tools and policy to detect and penalize work that isn't. Families navigating this moment should treat AI the way they would treat any other tool: useful for structure and support, never a substitute for a student's own thinking and story.
